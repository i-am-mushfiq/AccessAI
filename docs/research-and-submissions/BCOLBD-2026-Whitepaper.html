1 · Abstract

Bangladesh operates a large portfolio of social protection, education, health and livelihood programmes, but a citizen's ability to obtain one depends on knowing it exists, knowing whether they qualify, and being able to prove it. AccessAI is a working bilingual, voice-accessible platform in which a citizen describes a life event in ordinary Bangla and receives a deterministic, fully traced eligibility decision with cited evidence, an action plan and nearby offices. Language models render explanations; they never decide. Two limits are structural rather than incidental. First, the people who most need the system cannot read or type, and free-text understanding is currently pattern-based, so reach is capped by perception, not by rules — a genuine machine-learning problem. Second, the platform's own database is the only witness to what a programme's rule was and who verified it, so no ministry, NGO, auditor or citizen can check the operator's claim — a genuine multi-party trust problem. We propose an AI layer for Bangla speech, information extraction and circular-to-rule document intelligence, and a permissioned, token-free attestation ledger on which issuing organisations sign programme-rule versions and the platform anchors decision digests. Because eligibility is a pure function of committed inputs, an anchored decision is independently recomputable. No personal data, and no cryptoasset, is placed on-chain.

2 · Problem and context

2.1 Who experiences the problem, and what exactly goes wrong

The primary subject is a low-income citizen with limited formal literacy and a shared or low-specification phone: a widow in Rangpur, a flood-affected farmer in Kurigram, an elderly person or a person with a disability in a union outside a district town. Their difficulty is not one missing web page but a chain of four failures, and fixing only one changes nothing:

  1. Discovery. Programme rules live in circulars, ministry sites, notice boards and the working memory of local officials. There is no single place where "what am I entitled to, given what has just happened to me" is answerable.
  2. Interpretation. Eligibility is conjunctive and numeric — age bands, income ceilings, land holding, district coverage, household composition. A citizen cannot evaluate it from prose, and neither can a helpful neighbour.
  3. Proof. Even correct advice is unusable if the citizen cannot show that it was correct. Documents are re-collected for every programme, and a determination made at one office does not travel to the next.
  4. Accountability. When advice is wrong — a stale threshold, a superseded circular — nobody can establish afterwards what the rule was at the moment the advice was given, or who asserted it.

2.2 Why the problem persists

Three mechanisms keep it in place. Information asymmetry with a channel mismatch: the authoritative text is written for administrators, in a register and medium the beneficiary cannot use, while the citizen's only high-bandwidth channel is speech. Institutional fragmentation: the rules are owned by many separate bodies — Social Services, Women Affairs, Agricultural Extension, Youth Development, BMET, the National Legal Aid Services Organisation, and NGOs such as BRAC and BLAST — none with authority over the others' rules, and none an accepted custodian of a combined registry. Verification cost: confirming that a threshold is current is skilled human work, and without a process that makes it cheap and attributable any corpus decays into confident staleness.

2.3 Who else is affected, and what it costs

Field officers and Union Digital Centre operators absorb the interpretation burden as unpaid advisory work; programme administrators face inclusion and exclusion errors they cannot attribute to a cause; and development partners financing social protection cannot separate "the benefit did not reach people" from "people did not know about it". For a household the economic consequence is asymmetric: an unclaimed monthly allowance compounds over years, while a wasted trip to a district office costs a day's wage plus fare against a monthly income of a few thousand taka. The social consequence matters most for adoption — a citizen told "no" by a system that cannot explain itself experiences the state as having refused them.

2.4 Evidence position

Evidence discipline

This document deliberately asserts no numeric prevalence figure it cannot source. The qualitative claims above are grounded in named public frameworks — the National Social Security Strategy (2015), which itself identifies fragmentation across programmes and administering ministries as a core weakness; the a2i service ecosystem, the 333 national call centre and the Union Digital Centre network as the state's existing citizen-navigation channels; and BBS household survey instrumentation as the sampling frame any prevalence claim must come from. Section 20 lists the six measurements this project must produce before it is entitled to claim an impact number, and the baseline study is scheduled in Phase 2 rather than asserted now (Table 13).

2.5 Why current solutions are insufficient

The strongest existing alternatives are not other apps but a human helpline, a human operator and a general-purpose chatbot, and each fails on a different axis (Table 11). The 333 helpline and a UDC operator answer intelligibly over a channel citizens actually have, but neither leaves a written, traceable determination nor scales to per-citizen numeric evaluation across dozens of programmes. A general assistant is fluent in Bangla and useless here for one reason: it produces a plausible income threshold, and a plausible threshold is indistinguishable from a correct one to the person acting on it. Government portals hold the authoritative text but require the citizen to know what to look for. What is missing is a system that is simultaneously conversational, deterministic in its determinations, and externally verifiable in the rules it applies.

3 · The existing system, and where it stops

3.1 What is built today

AccessAI exists as a working modular monolith: one Next.js 15 application (React 19, TypeScript strict) over Drizzle ORM and libSQL, with 32 tables, 31 route files exposing 51 handlers under /api/v1, and 685 automated tests including 28 assertions that compute WCAG contrast ratios from the declared design tokens. It starts with two commands and needs no database server, no cache, and no API key. A feature discovery pass over the implementation catalogued 219 distinct capabilities across 19 domains, of which 201 are fully implemented in the default configuration, 11 are built but gated on an optional credential, and 7 are deliberately unbuilt with a stated fallback.

The citizen journey is complete end to end: sign in by phone, OTP and PIN; describe a situation in Bangla, English or Banglish by typing or speaking; receive ranked programmes with a per-condition eligibility verdict; save them, generate an action plan from the programme's own recorded application steps, tick tasks, and follow deadlines on a timeline; and find real nearby offices on an OpenStreetMap view. Staff surfaces are equally real: moderators author programmes and publish versioned rules, administrators verify records, and every staff mutation writes an append-only audit row.

3.2 The parts that carry the trust claim

Four implemented mechanisms matter for everything proposed later, because the AI and blockchain layers attach to them rather than replacing them.

  • Three-valued eligibility. The engine returns eligible, partially_eligible, not_eligible or unknown. A missing field yields unknown, never false, because treating absent data as failure silently denies people entitlements; a hard statutory bar still decides immediately, so a man is not asked pointless questions about a women-only allowance. Reasons are ordered by rule weight, so the citizen is told the deciding fact first. Rules are a validated JSON AST over 12 comparators with soft conditions, weights and declared requiredFields.
  • Replayable decisions. Every evaluation is stored with a full per-condition trace, the citizen's profile snapshot, and the rule version used. A decision shown in March is still explainable in June after both the income and the rule have changed.
  • Provenance and verification states. Records carry verificationStatus, source URL and note, last-verified date, verifier and review interval inline. A record is never born verified; verifying and editing cannot be one request; and editing a substantive field on a verified record revokes verification and re-indexes it. Confidence is capped by state — 65% unverified, 45% outdated, 25% disputed — and when the cap binds it is the first reason the citizen is shown.
  • Grounding observability. Every turn writes an AI log row — engine, model, prompt version, retrieved chunk ids, cited programme ids, confidence, tokens, latency, and a groundingFailure flag set when a recommendation had no citation behind it. The failure mode is queryable, not merely hoped absent.

3.3 Where the existing architecture is genuinely insufficient

The prototype is not weak in the places the next two sections address; it is complete in a way that makes its two real ceilings visible. Neither can be lifted by writing more of the same kind of code.

Table 2 · The two ceilings, and why more conventional engineering does not lift them
CeilingWhat actually happens todayWhy it is not a coding gap
Perception and recall — the reach ceiling Extraction is regex-based and deliberately conservative: an ambiguous value is not extracted, so recall is low on real speech — dialect, code-mixing, relational phrasing ("my husband's land"), local units (bigha, katha), and disfluency. Bangla stemming is shallow by choice, so lexical retrieval misses paraphrase. With no speech key and a non-Chromium browser, nothing can be heard at all. Each extra pattern raises recall on the phrasing you thought of and cannot generalise to the one you did not. Recognition and paraphrase matching are irreducibly statistical over open-ended input — a machine-learning problem, not an unwritten if.
Attestation — the trust ceiling The corpus is 42 programmes structured after real schemes whose thresholds are unverified; every record says so and confidence is capped at 65%. Lifting that cap is a single database write by one administrator of one operator. Provenance, verification and the audit log all live in the operator's own database. The mechanism is right and the custody is wrong: a log its own subject can rewrite is not evidence for that subject. No ministry can accept an operator's assertion of its rule, and no citizen or auditor can check it. A longer, signed or replicated log kept by the same operator does not change who is trusted.

Two further limits are honest context rather than technology gaps: the corpus is not yet verified against current circulars, which is verification labour and institutional authority rather than code; and the automated source fetcher is deliberately unbuilt, because a crawler that parses a threshold out of a PDF straight into a live corpus is one error away from telling a widow she qualifies for an allowance she does not. The safe order — fetch, checksum, diff, human review, publish — is exactly where document intelligence belongs (§7.4), and every stage of it except the extraction already exists.

4 · Proposed solution and central thesis

We keep the existing system as the core and add exactly two layers, each attached to one of the two ceilings.

  1. An AI layer that widens the input channel — Bangla speech recognition adapted to this domain and to regional speech, learned information extraction with per-field provenance and calibrated confidence, semantic retrieval, and document intelligence that turns circulars into proposed rule versions for human verification. Every one of these produces a proposal that a deterministic component or a person then confirms.
  2. A verification layer that moves custody of the trust claim off the operator — the Bhorosha Trust Registry: a permissioned, token-free ledger on which each issuing organisation signs an attestation binding a programme version and its rule AST to a content hash, and on which the platform periodically anchors Merkle roots over decision digests. Consumers verify signatures; they do not trust the operator's database.

The thesis. The one catastrophic failure mode is confidently wrong advice about an entitlement, and it has two independent causes needing different technologies: the system misunderstood the citizen, or the rule it applied was not what the issuing authority said. AI raises the ceiling on reach; the ledger raises the floor on accountability. They are joined by a property the existing code already has — because eligibility is a pure function of a profile snapshot and a rule AST, and both can be hash-committed, an anchored decision is independently recomputable by a third party. That property dies the moment a model decides anything, so "rules decide, AI explains" is not a slogan here: it is the precondition that makes the blockchain layer meaningful.

5 · Why AI, and where it is refused

5.1 The test we apply

A component earns the AI label here only if it maps an open-ended, high-variance input to a bounded, checkable output, and only if a deterministic implementation would fail by construction rather than for want of effort. By that test the current intent classifier, ranker, confidence scorer and voice command router are not AI and are not presented as such: each is a published formula or lexicon, and better for being one.

5.2 What we refuse to build, and why

  • No model in the eligibility decision. Not as a fallback, not as a tie-breaker. A deterministic rule over a hallucinated income yields a deterministic wrong answer carrying a confidence number — the precise trust failure the platform exists to prevent — and it would destroy the recomputability property the ledger layer depends on (§4).
  • No approval-likelihood prediction for an individual. There is no outcome dataset, because the platform does not submit applications; a score trained on nothing would discourage exactly the marginal applicant who most needs to apply. The ranker's "similar user success" factor therefore returns a neutral value and declares itself unavailable, and we propose keeping it so rather than fabricating a signal.
  • No fraud or risk scoring of citizens. The platform advises; it does not adjudicate. Scoring a citizen's honesty from their phrasing imports a discriminatory failure mode with no offsetting benefit. Abuse control stays rate limiting, lockout and audit.
  • No agentic auto-application. No tool-calling agent files a claim on a citizen's behalf. An autonomous action whose failure the citizen cannot see is not a service to a low-literacy user.
  • Automation labelled honestly. One planned improvement — choosing which missing field to ask about next by expected reduction in decision uncertainty across the candidate set — is decision-theoretic optimisation over the rule ASTs, not machine learning, and is described as such.

6 · Why blockchain, and where it is refused

6.1 The necessity test

We apply four conditions and require all four: (i) several mutually independent parties must write to the same state; (ii) no single party is an accepted custodian of it; (iii) readers must be able to detect tampering, omission or reordering without trusting the host; and (iv) the state is small and non-personal. One condition met is not a use case.

6.2 The strongest counter-argument, and our answer

The best objection is not "blockchain is unnecessary" but a specific alternative: let each ministry publish a digitally signed rule file on its own website, using the licensed certifying authorities that already operate under Bangladesh's ICT Act, and have consumers verify the signature — no ledger required. That alternative is strong, and it supplies something we do adopt: identity. It does not supply three things, and each is where a citizen is actually harmed.

  1. Non-equivocation. A signed file on a web server can be replaced, and one issuer can serve version A to one consumer and A′ to another. Both verify correctly. Only a shared, ordered, append-only log makes divergence detectable.
  2. Proof of absence. To act on a rule you must know it has not been superseded. A file offers no way to prove no later version exists; an ordered registry does.
  3. A namespace with no custodian. Fifteen bodies publishing in fifteen formats at fifteen URLs is the fragmentation we started with, relocated. Someone must hold the index — and who that is has been the political blocker all along.

The honest position, stated plainly: if one institution is willing to host the registry and every other issuer accepts its custody, a signed-record database with an append-only Merkle log is sufficient and cheaper, and we would recommend it. The ledger earns its place precisely because that acceptance does not exist across ministries and NGOs, and because a shared log lets them participate without conceding custody to a peer or to a startup. We therefore specify a permissioned network of named institutional members with no token — the minimum machinery that satisfies the requirement rather than the maximum the category name permits.

6.3 What the ledger changes for a citizen, concretely

The coupling to existing code is deliberately small. Today the 65% ceiling is lifted by one row edit inside the operator's database; under this proposal it lifts only when the client verifies an attestation signed by the organisation that owns the programme, and the confidence panel names the signer and the date. The screen the citizen already sees stops reporting an internal claim and starts reporting a checkable one. Second, the decision receipt carries a short code with which a UDC operator, caseworker or auditor can prove — with no personal data leaving the citizen's hands — that this decision, over this rule version, existed then and has not been altered since.

7 · Solution architecture

The proposed system is the existing application plus two side-cars. Nothing in the current request path is replaced, and every addition fails safe: without the AI layer the product degrades to what it does today, and with the ledger unreachable it serves the last verified attestation from cache and lowers its stated confidence rather than blocking a citizen.

8.2 Data, consent, residency and infrastructure

Public corpora are used under their licences; synthetic augmentation over dialect, units and disfluency covers the long tail at no privacy cost; citizen-derived data is used only where the citizen has opted in per purpose. Audio is never retained — it is transcribed inside the request handler and discarded, because speech about widowhood, income and illness is sensitive text plus a biometric identifier plus, often, other people audible in the room. A hosted rendering prompt carries only the fields the decision used, and the in-country self-hosted path exists so that a deployment which cannot accept cross-border transfer has an option other than switching the feature off. The pilot needs two application instances, one inference host for ASR, TTS and the extraction encoder, and libSQL with a documented migration to PostgreSQL and pgvector. The deterministic path answers in well under a second; live rendering measured 5.5 s server-side for a ~700-token Bangla reply against a 3-second target, which is why streaming is a named Phase 2 item rather than a claim. The batch corpus lane is queued and never contends with a citizen request.

Three properties then hold jointly: model output is always a proposal; every proposal meets a deterministic or human gate; and every gate failure has a defined destination — unknown for a field, the draft queue for a rule, the composer for prose — so no model failure produces silence or an unverifiable assertion.

9 · Blockchain architecture

9.1 Network, consensus and why not something heavier

The Bhorosha Trust Registry is a permissioned consortium ledger on Hyperledger Fabric, chosen for four properties rather than familiarity. Membership Service Providers map one-to-one onto real organisations, so an identity on the network is an institution rather than an anonymous address. Endorsement policies express multi-party approval natively, so "valid when the owning department endorses it and one independent reviewer co-endorses" is configuration, not application code. Channels isolate a body's drafts before publication. And Fabric has no native token and no gas — which matters, because a cryptoasset would put the project outside what Bangladesh Bank and the BFIU permit and make institutional participation impossible.

Ordering uses Raft across orderer nodes contributed by at least three distinct organisations. This is crash-fault-tolerant, not Byzantine-fault-tolerant, and we state the trade-off rather than obscuring it: among named, contractually bound public bodies the realistic threat is one compromised or careless member, which endorsement policies already contain, not a coalition rewriting history. Two mitigations cover the residual case. Ordering is spread across organisations, so no single member can censor; and the ledger head is anchored daily as one Merkle root transaction to a public chain, so even a colluding consortium cannot rewrite published history without the divergence being externally detectable. That anchor is the only place a public network appears, costs one small transaction per day, and is what makes the permissioned choice defensible rather than merely convenient. A public-only design was rejected in the other direction: attestation content is institutional, membership must be gated, and fees paid in a cryptoasset are not a lawful operating expense for a Bangladeshi public body.

9.2 What is on the ledger

Each per-decision digest is computed as HMAC(k_citizen, ruleHash ‖ contentHash ‖ profileSnapshotHash ‖ outcome ‖ timestamp). The keyed construction is not decoration: a plain hash over a small, low-entropy profile space is brute-forceable, so a per-citizen key held off-chain — and released only in the citizen's own receipt — is what prevents the anchor from becoming a re-identification oracle. This is the kind of detail where a naive design leaks exactly the population it was meant to protect.

9.3 Identity, permissions and legacy integration

Organisations, not people, hold on-chain identity. Each member has a Fabric MSP identity and a published DID (did:web under the organisation's own domain, so discovery needs no new infrastructure and revocation is under the organisation's control). Staff act through the signing console under role-based authority; the organisation's signing key lives in an HSM or a cloud KMS, never in the application. Citizens have no keys and sign nothing (§6.1, B8).

The practical objection — "ministries will not run nodes" — is met by separating three things usually conflated. Key custody always stays with the organisation. Node operation may be delegated to a technical operator such as the Bangladesh Computer Council under a service agreement, so a body can be a full issuer without running infrastructure. Attestation authoring happens through a web console, a CSV upload, or a signed-document workflow mirroring how these bodies already approve papers. Legacy integration is deliberately undemanding: consumers read the registry through a REST gateway with webhooks, so MyGov or a UDC operator tool integrates with no blockchain client, and where a body cannot sign yet the platform runs exactly as today with the honest 65% ceiling. Adoption is incremental per organisation, not all-or-nothing.

9.4 Errors, reversals and disputes

Immutability is a property to design around, not a slogan. Nothing is ever deleted or edited on the ledger; correction is by append. A wrong attestation is corrected by the issuer publishing a withdrawn statement and a superseding version, which changes the answer the client computes from that moment forward while leaving the record of the error intact — which is the point, since the error is exactly what an auditor needs to see. A citizen's dispute enters the existing feedback and moderation queue, and a substantiated dispute becomes a disputed attestation that drops the confidence ceiling to 25% while remaining visible. Because no value, entitlement or payment is represented on-chain, there is no financial transaction to reverse: the ledger records claims about rules, and the authoritative operational state — profiles, decisions, plans — stays in the off-chain database where it can be corrected, and erased, normally.

10 · On-chain / off-chain design

The placement rule is a single sentence: data goes on-chain only if a party who does not trust the host must be able to verify it, and only if it is small, non-personal and stable. Everything else stays off-chain. Applied honestly, that leaves the ledger holding a few hundred bytes per record and the database holding the entire product.

11 · Privacy and security

11.1 What is already enforced

Authentication is phone, OTP and PIN, because much of the target population has no email address: Argon2id with a self-describing scrypt fallback, 15-minute access and 30-day refresh tokens, both httpOnly, with rotation and reuse detection that revokes a whole session family when an already-rotated token appears. Authorisation is checked twice — at the edge and again in every mutation handler, because a 15-minute token can outlive a demotion — and a suspended account is treated as signed out while its token is still valid. Token-bucket limits are 10/min for auth, 20/min for AI, 120/min otherwise; every boundary input is schema-parsed; health data persists only on explicit opt-in; every staff mutation writes an append-only audit row with actor, role, before and after; and a production boot is refused if a signing secret is still the shipped development value or OTP echo is on.

11.3 Data protection posture

The lawful basis is consent, captured per purpose and per field, with health data off by default and a working deletion path. Minimisation is structural rather than promised: the prompt carries only the fields the decision used, and the ledger carries no personal data at all. Bangladesh's data protection regime is still consolidating — successive drafts of a Personal Data Protection Act have circulated, and the Cyber Security Act 2023 governs adjacent obligations — so the design takes the stricter plausible outcome: in-country processing available for every model, cross-border transfer only under a data processing agreement with retention disabled, and a documented impact assessment before any real-citizen deployment. One item stays unresolved rather than assumed away: a retention period for conversations and evaluations, and whether an audit entry naming a staff member survives that person's own deletion request — a genuine tension between accountability and erasure for an operator and a regulator to settle, not an engineering choice.

12 · Governance

Governance is the part of a consortium design that fails quietly, so it is specified as a charter with named thresholds rather than as principles — and nothing in it implies that decentralisation removes legal responsibility.

Table 9 · Governance model across the four domains
DomainQuestionAnswer under the proposed charter
Network membershipWho may join, and in what class?Three classes. Issuers — public bodies and licensed NGOs owning programme rules; they alone may attest. Consumers — applications and operator networks with read access and the right to anchor their own decisions. Technical operators — hosts with no attestation rights. Admission needs a two-thirds vote of issuers, on evidence of legal mandate.
Who validates and operates nodes?Endorsing peers are run by issuers or their delegated operators; ordering spans at least three organisations. Key custody never transfers with node operation (§9.3).
Who may be suspended — or leave?Two-thirds vote suspends an issuer for signing outside its mandate or a key failure; suspension blocks new attestations and leaves history intact, since deletion would destroy the audit value. Business rulesWho sets vocabulary and review intervals?A standards committee of issuers, ratified by two-thirds. Defaults inherit the implemented model: 180-day intervals, five verification states, published ceilings.
Who resolves disputes, and who may override?A panel of three — an issuer not party to it, a consumer, an independent member — within a published SLA, executing its ruling as an append: withdraw, supersede, or mark disputed. Nobody overrides on-chain. Off-chain a caseworker may record a manual determination, flagged as such, because real circumstances outrun any rule set and a system that cannot say "a human decided this" gets worked around.
How are programmes and partners added?An issuer registers versions inside its own mandate without a vote; a new mandate boundary goes through admission.
TechnologyWho owns the infrastructure?No single party: peers and orderers are contributed by members, and a neutral operator — the Bangladesh Computer Council is the natural in-country candidate — may run nodes under contract for those who cannot.
How is software upgraded?Chaincode upgrades need an independent security review, two-thirds endorsement, a notice period and an on-chain record. Application releases are the operator's business, not the consortium's.
Keys, recovery, security ownership?Each member holds its keys in an HSM or KMS with documented rotation and a break-glass path, and owns its keys and nodes; the operator owns the application, citizen data and the anchor service; the consortium owns chaincode review and incident coordination, with a named contact per member. A lost registry rebuilds from members' signed attestations.
RegulatoryHow does oversight coexist with a ledger?A regulator or auditor holds a read-only node: full visibility of attestations and anchors, no write or endorsement rights, no access to citizen data — which is off-chain and reachable only through the lawful-request process. Stronger than a status quo where the regulator sees only what an operator exports.
Where does legal responsibility sit?Where it sits today. The operator is data controller for citizen data; each issuer is accountable for its own attestations; the consortium is a coordination body, not a shield, and no participant may cite decentralisation as a defence.

15 · Business model, revenue and distribution

Who pays. Never the citizen, for anything in the guidance path. Four institutional payers, in the order they become available: NGOs and development-partner projects (licence plus deployment); government departments (service contract for registry operation and integrations); consumer applications (metered read access to the attestation API); and donor-funded verification programmes, paying for corpus verification as a public good. The chain from technology to revenue is short and every link is testable: AI widens the channel → more citizens reach a correct determination → an institution's cost per assisted citizen falls and targeting improves → it pays for the tooling and the evidence; the ledger makes guidance attributable → a department can lend its name to it → a service contract becomes possible at all. Without the ledger the last link does not close, which is the commercial argument for it.

20 · Expected impact and how it will be measured

We publish no projected number of citizens reached: the platform cannot observe outcomes it does not mediate, so any "successful application" figure would be self-reported at an unknown response rate. Six measurements are instead committed to in Phase 2, each instrumentable with what is already logged:

  1. Failure-stage baseline. Structured interviews with applicants and rejected applicants at a Union Digital Centre, reporting where the process breaks — unaware, aware but ineligible, or eligible but defeated by the process. This decides whether the premise holds.
  2. Determination accuracy. Agreement between the engine's verdict and a caseworker's independent determination on the same profile, per programme.
  3. Unknown-rate reduction. The share of evaluations returning unknown before and after learned extraction — the direct measure of whether the AI layer widened the channel.
  4. Reviewer minutes per verified programme version, before and after document intelligence.
  5. Assisted-session duration at a UDC counter, against the operator's current baseline.
  6. Verification coverage and freshness — the share of the corpus with a valid attestation and the median age of the attestation, published continuously rather than claimed once.

The impact we do claim is structural: a citizen today receives advice whose basis nobody can check, and after Phase 3 receives advice carrying a signature from the body that owns the rule. That change is verifiable on the screen, and it is the one this project exists to make.

21 · Conclusion

The problem is not that Bangladeshi citizens lack a website. It is that entitlement knowledge is fragmented across institutions, written in a register its intended beneficiaries cannot read, and unattributable when it turns out to be wrong. AccessAI already answers the middle of that: a working, tested, bilingual system in which rules decide, a language model only explains, and every determination is traced, cited and replayable. The two things it cannot do alone are what this proposal adds. AI opens the channel to citizens who speak rather than type, and lifts the verification bottleneck that decides whether the corpus is ever true. A permissioned, token-free attestation ledger moves the trust claim out of the operator's database into signatures from the bodies that own the rules — and because eligibility is a pure function of hash-committed inputs, an anchored decision becomes independently recomputable rather than merely stored. Neither technology is here because a category asked for it: statistical perception cannot be written as rules, and non-equivocation cannot be self-certified by the party under scrutiny. Where a conventional alternative would suffice, this paper says so and recommends it.
